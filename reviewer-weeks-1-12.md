## Week 1 — Entering the world of literature

**Focus:** What we read, how we read, and why it matters.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Great Books study asks what stories mean, how form shapes meaning, and whose experiences a literary tradition includes. Reading well is active work: notice, question, support an interpretation, and stay open to more than one lens.</p>
      <h3>1.1 What is literature?</h3><p><strong>Genre</strong> is a broad kind of writing. The course groups works as <strong>fiction</strong> (invented narratives), <strong>nonfiction</strong> (claims about real people/events), <strong>poetry</strong> (language shaped by rhythm, sound, imagery, and compression), and <strong>drama</strong> (writing intended for performance). A work can blend forms.</p><p>A <strong>canon</strong> is a changing, curated body of works a culture treats as especially influential or worth studying. The “Great Conversation” is the long exchange among writers and readers across time. Ask who selected the canon, whose voices were left out, and how later writers answer earlier texts. <strong>Universality</strong> describes themes that resonate across different eras and cultures; it does not mean every reader experiences a book in the same way.</p>
      <h3>1.2 Reading through critical lenses</h3><p>A lens highlights some features of a text. It does not replace close reading.</p><div class="table-scroll"><table><thead><tr><th>Lens</th><th>Ask</th><th>Clue</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Formalism</td><td>How do structure, imagery, word choice, irony, and repetition create meaning?</td><td>Evidence stays inside the text.</td></tr><tr><td>Marxism</td><td>How do class, wealth, labor, and power shape characters and outcomes?</td><td>Who owns, works, benefits, or is exploited?</td></tr><tr><td>Feminism</td><td>How are gender, agency, voice, and unequal expectations represented?</td><td>Whose choices are limited or taken seriously?</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Other lenses include historical, biographical, psychoanalytic, postcolonial, and reader-response approaches. Use a claim plus textual evidence; do not treat a lens as a label that proves itself.</p>
      <h3>1.3 Why read deeply?</h3><p><strong>Deep reading</strong> is sustained, attentive reading that lets you follow complex ideas, notice ambiguity, and revise your first impression. It is different from skimming for a keyword. Literature can build <strong>empathy</strong> by asking you to imagine another person’s perspective, though reading alone does not guarantee moral agreement or understanding. <strong>Universal questions</strong>—love, justice, death, belonging, identity, power—connect works without erasing their specific cultures and histories.</p><div class="note"><strong>Study move:</strong> After a passage, write “I notice… / The text shows… / This may mean…” Keep observation, evidence, and interpretation distinct.</div>

### Works and stories

- **The Great Conversation — Course concept.** A metaphor for the continuing exchange among writers, ideas, and readers across centuries. Later works may imitate, question, or revise earlier ones; “conversation” does not mean all traditions share one origin.
- **Cinderella — Folktale tradition.** A mistreated young woman receives help to attend a royal gathering, where a lost shoe helps identify her. Versions differ widely; comparing them reveals how family, gender, work, and justice change across cultures.

## Week 2 — Approaches to reading great books

**Focus:** Annotate, contextualize, then make an evidence-based claim.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Good literary analysis is not a guess about what the author “really meant.” It is a reasoned interpretation built from careful reading and relevant context.</p>
      <h3>2.1 Active reading and annotation</h3><p><strong>Annotation</strong> means making useful notes while reading: mark a confusing phrase, ask a question, notice a repeated image, record a character change, or summarize a paragraph in the margin. Highlighting can help you locate a passage, but it is not analysis by itself. <strong>Passive scanning</strong> moves over the page looking for a term without building a connected understanding. A short margin summary is a kind of marginal note or marginalia.</p>
      <h3>2.2 Context: the world around a text</h3><div class="table-scroll"><table><thead><tr><th>Context</th><th>What it asks</th><th>Example</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Historical</td><td>What events and conditions shaped the period?</td><td>A novel written just after World War II.</td></tr><tr><td>Biographical</td><td>What relevant experiences of the author illuminate the work?</td><td>An author’s childhood shaping a character’s memories.</td></tr><tr><td>Cultural</td><td>What beliefs, institutions, customs, and expectations shaped the audience?</td><td>Gender roles in the nineteenth century.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Context is a tool, not a shortcut. The text may confirm, resist, or complicate what you know about its period. Avoid claiming that an author’s life directly explains every fictional choice.</p>
      <h3>2.3 From observation to interpretation</h3><p>A <strong>fact</strong> can be checked directly in the text (“the protagonist dies”). An <strong>interpretation</strong> explains what a detail suggests (“the death exposes the failure of the American Dream”). Strong analysis names the feature, gives a specific quotation or scene, and explains how the evidence supports the claim. A thesis is a concise, debatable interpretation—not a plot recap.</p><div class="note"><strong>Exam formula:</strong> State the idea → name one detail → explain the connection. If the question asks “what happens,” summarize; if it asks “what does it mean,” interpret.</div>

### Works and stories

- **The Great Gatsby — F. Scott Fitzgerald.** Narrator Nick Carraway recounts the lavish world of Jay Gatsby, who hopes to reunite with Daisy Buchanan. Daisy’s car kills Myrtle Wilson; Myrtle’s husband George later shoots Gatsby and then himself. Nick leaves disillusioned with the carelessness, class privilege, and failed dream around him.
- **The Catcher in the Rye — J. D. Salinger.** Teenager Holden Caulfield recounts leaving school and wandering New York after expulsion. He considers running away, but his younger sister Phoebe’s presence helps him reconsider; watching her ride the carousel gives him a rare moment of acceptance. Holden tells the story from a care facility, and his selective voice reveals grief, loneliness, and fear of adulthood.

## Week 3 — Greek and Roman foundations

**Focus:** Philosophy, tragedy, public duty, and the good life.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Greek and Roman texts ask how people can know what is true, live well, and meet obligations to other people and the state.</p>
      <h3>3.1 Plato, Aristotle, and Greek tragedy</h3><p><strong>Plato</strong> uses the contrast between changing appearances and ideal <strong>Forms</strong>; the famous Cave image asks how education can turn a person toward knowledge. <strong>Aristotle’s Poetics</strong> studies how plot and character work in tragedy. <strong>Sophocles’ Oedipus Rex</strong> stages the danger of trying to outrun prophecy and the shock of discovering the truth. <strong>Hubris</strong> is excessive pride or overconfidence; it can contribute to error, but it is not identical to every tragic mistake.</p>
      <h3>3.2 Roman duty and public speech</h3><p>In Virgil’s <strong>Aeneid</strong>, Aeneas carries the survivors of Troy toward a destined homeland. His Roman virtue is <strong>pietas</strong>—duty toward family, gods, and community—even when duty conflicts with private desire. <strong>Rhetoric</strong> is the art of persuasive public speech. <strong>Gravitas</strong> suggests seriousness, dignity, and responsibility in public life.</p>
      <h3>3.3 Stoicism and human flourishing</h3><p><strong>Stoicism</strong>, practiced by Roman thinkers including Marcus Aurelius, trains attention on what is within one’s control: judgments, choices, and conduct. <em>Meditations</em> is a private notebook of reminders, not a plot-driven book. <strong>Didactic literature</strong> aims to teach or instruct. <strong>Eudaimonia</strong> means human flourishing or a well-lived life, not simply pleasure or wealth.</p><div class="note"><strong>Connect the terms:</strong> Aeneas models responsibility to a larger duty; Marcus Aurelius asks how to keep one’s judgment steady; Sophocles asks what a person can know and control.</div>

### Works and stories

- **The Republic — Plato.** A philosophical dialogue that asks what justice is and imagines a well-ordered city. The Allegory of the Cave distinguishes shadows from knowledge and uses education as a turning toward truth.
- **Poetics — Aristotle.** A compact study of poetic forms, especially tragedy. Aristotle analyzes plot, recognition, reversal, and catharsis as elements that shape an audience’s experience.
- **Oedipus Rex — Sophocles.** A plague afflicts Thebes, and King Oedipus investigates the old king’s murder. Evidence shows that Oedipus himself killed his father and married his mother, unknowingly fulfilling prophecy; Jocasta dies and Oedipus blinds himself.
- **Antigone — Sophocles.** After civil war, Creon forbids burial for Polyneices, but Antigone performs the rites, believing divine and family obligations outweigh the king’s command. Creon reverses himself too late; Antigone, Haemon, and Eurydice die, exposing the cost of inflexible power.
- **Aeneid — Virgil.** The Trojan survivor Aeneas leads his people toward Italy, a future homeland. He loves Dido in Carthage but leaves to fulfill his duty; Dido dies by suicide. The epic follows war and sacrifice on the path to Rome’s mythic origins.
- **Meditations — Marcus Aurelius.** Private reflections by a Roman emperor practicing Stoicism. The notes return to virtue, mortality, self-command, and the difference between what one can and cannot control.

## Week 4 — Oral tradition and mythology

**Focus:** Gilgamesh, shared myth patterns, and the hero’s journey.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Stories passed by voice preserve shared memory, but each retelling can change details. Myths use memorable figures and events to explore origins, danger, power, and human limits.</p>
      <h3>4.1 The Epic of Gilgamesh</h3><p>The <strong>Epic of Gilgamesh</strong> is among the earliest surviving written epics, based on older Mesopotamian story traditions. King Gilgamesh begins arrogant and abusive. The gods create <strong>Enkidu</strong>, a wild man who becomes his friend. They face Humbaba and the Bull of Heaven; after Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh’s grief drives him to seek immortality. He meets Utnapishtim, who survived a great flood, but Gilgamesh fails the tests for eternal life and loses a rejuvenating plant to a serpent. He returns understanding that a mortal’s legacy and care for a city matter more than escaping death.</p><p><strong>Oral tradition</strong> is transmission by spoken performance across generations. A written version can preserve one version of a story without ending its oral life.</p>
      <h3>4.2 Compare myths carefully</h3><p>A <strong>creation myth</strong> explains beginnings; a <strong>flood myth</strong> tells of catastrophic waters and survival. Examples in the lesson include Genesis and Noah, Utnapishtim in Gilgamesh, Manu in Hindu tradition, the Egyptian Ennead, and the Philippine Malakas and Maganda story. Similar motifs invite comparison, but do not assume cultures share the same beliefs or that one version is the “original” of all others. A <strong>trickster</strong> bends rules through cunning or mischief (for example, Anansi or Hermes). An <strong>archetype</strong> is a recurring character-pattern or image, such as the questing hero.</p>
      <h3>4.3 The hero’s journey</h3><p>Joseph Campbell popularized a comparative pattern called the <strong>monomyth</strong>: a hero leaves the familiar world, faces trials, and returns changed. The course’s three broad stages are <strong>Departure, Initiation, Return</strong>. A <strong>Call to Adventure</strong> invites the hero out of ordinary life; a <strong>Mentor</strong> provides guidance or a tool; the <strong>Return with the Elixir</strong> brings knowledge or a gift back to the community. This is a useful comparison, not a rule every culture’s story must follow.</p><p>Try it with Gilgamesh, Odysseus, Hercules, or modern examples such as Harry Potter, Star Wars, and Katniss Everdeen: identify the specific scene rather than forcing every beat to fit.</p>

### Works and stories

- **Epic of Gilgamesh — Anonymous Mesopotamian tradition.** King Gilgamesh’s friendship with Enkidu softens his arrogance. After Enkidu dies, Gilgamesh searches for immortality, meets flood survivor Utnapishtim, fails to defeat sleep, and loses a life-restoring plant to a serpent. He returns to Uruk facing mortality and valuing human legacy.
- **Genesis: Noah and the Flood — Hebrew Bible tradition.** In the biblical flood narrative, Noah builds an ark to preserve his family and animals through a catastrophic flood. After the waters recede, a covenant and rainbow signify a renewed relationship; traditions interpret its meaning in different ways.
- **Malakas and Maganda — Philippine creation-story tradition.** In a well-known Philippine origin story, a bird pecks open a bamboo stalk and a man and woman emerge. The tale’s versions vary; it connects human beginnings with local landscape, animals, and the bamboo plant.
- **The Odyssey — Homeric tradition.** After the Trojan War, Odysseus struggles for years to return to Ithaca. He survives monsters, temptations, and storms, then reunites with his son and wife Penelope and restores his household.
- **The Hunger Games — Suzanne Collins.** Katniss Everdeen volunteers for her younger sister in a televised contest where children must fight to the death. Her choices with Peeta expose the Capitol’s cruelty and turn her into a symbol of resistance.
- **Manu and the Great Flood — Satapatha Brahmana and later Hindu traditions.** In one Vedic version, Manu shelters a small fish that grows large and warns him of a coming flood. Manu builds a boat, and the fish guides him to safety. Later tellings identify the fish with Vishnu’s Matsya avatar and add other survivors; versions differ, so do not collapse every Hindu retelling into one fixed plot. <a href="https://msrvvp.ac.in/ved-vidya/30_Vedavidya.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Research on the Manu flood traditions ↗</a>
- **Heliopolitan Creation Myth (the Ennead) — Ancient Egyptian religious tradition.** This creation account begins with Atum emerging from the primeval waters. Atum produces Shu and Tefnut; their descendants include Geb and Nut, then Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys. The Ennead means a group of nine deities, though sources and membership vary; it is one Egyptian creation tradition among several, not the only Egyptian account. <a href="https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/4829/files/Johansen_uchicago_0330D_16525.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">University of Chicago research on Egyptian cosmogonies ↗</a>

## Week 5 — Medieval worlds: allegory, chivalry, and epic

**Focus:** Everyman, Arthurian romance, Beowulf, and Dante’s Inferno.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Medieval works often place an individual inside a moral, religious, and communal order. Their symbols and heroic codes make abstract questions visible through people and choices.</p>
      <h3>5.1 Allegory and mortality</h3><p><strong>Allegory</strong> uses characters or events to represent broader moral, spiritual, or political ideas. <strong>Memento mori</strong> means “remember that you must die”; it calls people to consider how they use limited time. In the morality play <strong>Everyman</strong>, Death summons the main character to account for his life. Fellowship, family, possessions, and strength abandon him; Good Deeds remains his final companion. The play makes the lesson concrete: worldly status cannot substitute for moral action.</p>
      <h3>5.2 Arthurian legend and chivalry</h3><p><strong>Chivalry</strong> is an idealized code of knightly behavior involving courage, loyalty, courtesy, and service. Arthur’s Round Table imagines fellowship and shared honor, while the stories reveal the code’s limits. In many Grail versions, Lancelot’s love for Queen Guinevere and betrayal of Arthur’s trust make him unworthy to complete the Grail quest; Galahad is the knight who achieves it. Thomas Malory’s <em>Le Morte d’Arthur</em> gathers Arthur’s rise, the fellowship, rivalries, the Grail, and the kingdom’s collapse.</p>
      <h3>5.3 Beowulf and Dante</h3><p>The anonymous Old English epic <strong>Beowulf</strong> follows its hero through three battles: he defeats Grendel, kills Grendel’s mother in her underwater lair, and—after ruling for fifty years—faces a dragon. He and the loyal Wiglaf kill it, but Beowulf dies of his wounds; his people mourn and fear for the future. The poem asks what courage and leadership can protect, and what they cannot.</p><p>In Dante Alighieri’s <strong>Inferno</strong>, the pilgrim Dante is lost in a dark wood. The Roman poet <strong>Virgil</strong> guides him down through Hell’s nine circles, where punishments reflect the sins; this pattern is often called <strong>contrapasso</strong>. Dante encounters historical and legendary figures, including Paolo and Francesca among the lustful, tossed by an unending storm. At the bottom he sees Satan frozen in ice. The journey is a moral and political vision, not simply a travel adventure. <a href="https://richmond.la.utexas.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/9_Sept-28-Dante-1010-1039.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">University of Texas at Austin guide to Dante’s Comedy ↗</a> · <a href="https://assets.cambridge.org/97805211/00625/excerpt/9780521100625_excerpt.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cambridge overview of Beowulf ↗</a></p><div class="note"><strong>Do not mix the guides:</strong> Virgil guides Dante through Hell and Purgatory; Beatrice guides him later in Paradise.</div>

### Works and stories

- **Everyman — Anonymous English morality play.** Death tells Everyman to prepare for judgment. Friends, relatives, beauty, strength, and wealth abandon him; Good Deeds accompanies him to the grave, showing what the play considers spiritually lasting.
- **Le Morte d’Arthur — Thomas Malory.** Malory gathers legends of Arthur’s rise, the Round Table, knightly quests, Lancelot and Guinevere’s forbidden love, and the Grail. Betrayal and conflict divide the fellowship; Arthur and Mordred mortally wound one another, and the kingdom collapses.
- **Beowulf — Anonymous Old English poet.** Beowulf kills Grendel and Grendel’s mother to defend Hrothgar’s hall. Decades later, now king, he slays a dragon with Wiglaf’s help but dies from his wounds, leaving the Geats vulnerable. Cambridge’s scholarly introduction summarizes the three-part plot.
- **Inferno — Dante Alighieri.** Lost in a dark wood, the pilgrim Dante is led by Virgil through Hell’s nine circles, where punishments mirror sins. He sees famous souls, passes frozen Satan at the bottom, and emerges toward the mountain of Purgatory. The poem combines spiritual allegory with political judgments about Dante’s Italy. <a href="https://richmond.la.utexas.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/09/9_Sept-28-Dante-1010-1039.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">University of Texas at Austin guide ↗</a>
- **Arthurian Grail romances — Chrétien de Troyes and later writers.** A cycle of romances follows knights such as Lancelot, Gawain, and Perceval through tests of courage, loyalty, and spiritual worth. In many versions, Lancelot’s love for Guinevere prevents him from achieving the Grail, while Galahad succeeds.

## Week 7 — Renaissance and Enlightenment

**Focus:** Human potential, Shakespeare’s stage, satire, and rights.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Renaissance and Enlightenment writers debate human potential, authority, reason, religious tradition, and the rights people deserve.</p>
      <h3>7.1 Renaissance humanism and Shakespeare</h3><p><strong>Renaissance humanism</strong> renews attention to classical learning, education, human capacities, and life in this world. Shakespeare’s Globe Theatre became a famous London performance space. A <strong>soliloquy</strong> is a speech delivered alone on stage that lets the audience hear a character’s thinking.</p><p>In <strong>Hamlet</strong>, Denmark’s Prince Hamlet learns from his father’s ghost that King Claudius murdered him. Hamlet delays, tests the accusation with a play, and accidentally kills Polonius; Ophelia dies, and her brother Laertes seeks revenge. In the final fencing match, poison kills Gertrude, Laertes, and Hamlet; Hamlet kills Claudius before dying. “<strong>To be, or not to be</strong>” is Hamlet’s Act 3, Scene 1 soliloquy about whether to endure life’s suffering or end it, and about the fear of what may follow death. It is not just a slogan for indecision. <a href="https://shakespeare.folger.edu/downloads/pdf/hamlet_PDF_FolgerShakespeare.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Folger edition, synopsis, and full text ↗</a></p>
      <h3>7.2 Satire and the Age of Reason</h3><p><strong>Satire</strong> uses irony, exaggeration, humor, or parody to expose social or political faults. Jonathan Swift’s <strong>A Modest Proposal</strong> adopts a calm economic voice to propose selling and eating poor Irish infants; the outrageous proposal attacks the dehumanizing treatment of the poor and English exploitation. It is not a sincere policy plan.</p><p>The <strong>Enlightenment</strong>—also called the Age of Reason—emphasizes reason, evidence, debate, and reform, though thinkers disagreed about how far those principles should extend. In Voltaire’s <strong>Candide</strong>, a sheltered young man is pushed through war, disaster, exploitation, and loss while his teacher Pangloss insists that all is for the best. Candide’s final commitment to “cultivate our garden” favors practical work over empty optimism.</p>
      <h3>7.3 Rights and secular thought</h3><p>Thomas Paine’s <strong>Rights of Man</strong> argues in defense of popular political rights and the French Revolution. Mary Wollstonecraft’s <strong>A Vindication of the Rights of Woman</strong> argues that women appear inferior largely because education and opportunity have been denied to them; it calls for rational education and equality. <strong>Secularism</strong> gives worldly reason and public life an independent role from religious authority. It does not simply mean that every secular person is atheist.</p>

### Works and stories

- **Hamlet — William Shakespeare.** After the Ghost tells Hamlet that Claudius murdered his father, Hamlet stages a play to test the charge, then delays and causes more deaths. The tragedy ends with poisoned blades, the deaths of Hamlet, Laertes, Gertrude, and Claudius, and Fortinbras arriving to claim Denmark. The “To be or not to be” soliloquy considers suffering, death, and the unknowable afterlife. <a href="https://shakespeare.folger.edu/downloads/pdf/hamlet_PDF_FolgerShakespeare.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Folger synopsis and text ↗</a>
- **A Modest Proposal — Jonathan Swift.** Swift’s speaker proposes that impoverished Irish families sell infants as food to wealthy people. The appalling, businesslike suggestion is satire: it exposes the cruelty of treating human beings as economic burdens and condemns indifference to poverty.
- **Candide — Voltaire.** Candide travels through war, earthquake, exploitation, and personal loss while Pangloss insists everything is for the best. Experience undermines easy optimism; Candide’s final choice to cultivate a garden values useful work and care over grand abstractions.
- **Rights of Man — Thomas Paine.** A political argument defending natural rights and representative government, written in response to the French Revolution and Edmund Burke. Paine rejects inherited privilege and makes political legitimacy answerable to living people.
- **A Vindication of the Rights of Woman — Mary Wollstonecraft.** Wollstonecraft argues that women seem less rational largely because society denies them education and independence. She calls for equal intellectual development and for relationships grounded in reason and respect.

## Week 8 — The rise and craft of the novel

**Focus:** Picaresque, point of view, and character change.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Novels create the impression of a full world through narrative voice, believable detail, and characters who meet pressure over time.</p>
      <h3>8.1 Don Quixote, picaresque, and realism</h3><p>Miguel de Cervantes’s <strong>Don Quixote</strong> begins with Alonso Quixano, a country gentleman so absorbed in chivalric romances that he renames himself Don Quixote and sets out as a knight-errant. His practical neighbor <strong>Sancho Panza</strong> becomes his squire, hoping for a reward. Quixote sees windmills as giants, inns as castles, and ordinary encounters as quests; the mismatch between his ideals and the world creates comedy and pathos. After repeated defeats, he returns home, regains his reason, rejects chivalric books, and dies as Alonso Quixano. His ending complicates the comic adventures: the story asks what imagination can change, and what it cannot. <a href="https://assets.cambridge.org/97805213/13452/excerpt/9780521313452_excerpt.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cambridge University Press on Don Quixote ↗</a></p><p>A <strong>picaresque</strong> follows a roguish, low-status protagonist through episodic adventures and social worlds. <strong>Verisimilitude</strong> is the appearance of truth or plausibility in a work of fiction; it is an effect, not proof that a story is factual.</p>
      <h3>8.2 Point of view and reliability</h3><p><strong>Point of view</strong> is the position from which a story is told. A first-person narrator tells their own account; an omniscient narrator can report the thoughts of many characters and events beyond one person’s knowledge. An <strong>unreliable narrator</strong> gives an account readers have reason to question because of bias, limited knowledge, self-deception, or contradiction. Holden Caulfield in <em>The Catcher in the Rye</em> and Nick Carraway in <em>The Great Gatsby</em> interpret events through their own voices; a narrator’s account is not identical to the author’s verdict.</p>
      <h3>8.3 Character and development</h3><p>The <strong>protagonist</strong> is the central character whose goals and conflicts organize the story; the protagonist need not be morally good. A <strong>dynamic character</strong> undergoes a significant inner change. A <strong>bildungsroman</strong> is a coming-of-age or development novel. In Charlotte Brontë’s <strong>Jane Eyre</strong>, an orphan survives a harsh childhood, gains education, becomes a governess, and loves Edward Rochester. When she discovers he has a living wife hidden at Thornfield, Jane leaves rather than sacrifice her principles. An inheritance gives her independence; she returns after Thornfield burns and Rochester is injured, and they marry on more equal terms.</p>

### Works and stories

- **Don Quixote — Miguel de Cervantes.** Alonso Quixano reads so many tales of knights that he becomes Don Quixote, with Sancho Panza as his squire. He sees windmills as giants and ordinary inns as castles. After repeated defeats, he returns home, regains his reason, renounces chivalric romances, and dies as Alonso Quixano. The comic misreadings ask whether idealism can challenge a compromised reality. <a href="https://assets.cambridge.org/97805213/13452/excerpt/9780521313452_excerpt.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cambridge University Press overview ↗</a>
- **Jane Eyre — Charlotte Brontë.** Orphan Jane grows into an independent teacher and governess. She loves Rochester but leaves when she learns he has a living wife; after inheriting money and returning to find him injured and Thornfield destroyed, she chooses a relationship on more equal terms.
- **Pride and Prejudice — Jane Austen.** Elizabeth Bennet initially misjudges the proud Mr Darcy, while he must confront his own class prejudice. New evidence revises both judgments; their eventual marriage follows mutual respect and self-knowledge.
- **The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — Arthur Conan Doyle.** A collection of detective cases narrated mostly by Dr Watson. Holmes solves puzzles through observation and inference; Watson’s limited perspective controls what the reader learns and builds suspense.

## Week 9 — Romanticism, the Victorian age, and empire

**Focus:** The sublime, social reform, Gothic fears, and colonial power.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Literature changes as people argue over nature, industrial life, social inequality, and the stories an empire tells about itself.</p>
      <h3>9.1 Romanticism and the sublime</h3><p><strong>Romanticism</strong> values imagination, emotion, individual experience, and the power of nature. William Wordsworth calls poetry the “spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings” recollected in tranquility. The <strong>sublime</strong> is an encounter with vastness or power that inspires awe and may also cause fear. A <strong>Byronic hero</strong> is a brooding, defiant, isolated figure associated with Lord Byron’s poetry; later characters such as Heathcliff in Emily Brontë’s <em>Wuthering Heights</em> and Rochester in <em>Jane Eyre</em> can echo the type.</p><div class="note"><strong>Reading note:</strong> The module invokes Wordsworth to explain Romanticism but does not name a particular poem; check your assigned reading before memorizing a title.</div>
      <h3>9.2 Victorian fiction and Gothic anxiety</h3><p>The <strong>Victorian Era</strong> broadly refers to the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), a period of industrial growth, urban poverty, reform, and imperial expansion. Charles Dickens’s <strong>Oliver Twist</strong> follows an orphan abused in a workhouse who escapes to London and is drawn into Fagin’s gang; kindness and family ties eventually help rescue him. <strong>Hard Times</strong> criticizes an industrial town’s worship of “Facts” and utilitarian discipline by showing their damage to imagination and human feeling.</p><p><strong>Gothic fiction</strong> uses fear, secrecy, the uncanny, and threatening settings to dramatize cultural anxieties. Bram Stoker’s <strong>Dracula</strong> assembles letters and diaries as a group tries to stop the vampire Count. Robert Louis Stevenson’s <strong>Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde</strong> follows a doctor whose experiments divide respectable identity from violent impulses. Gothic is a mode, not just any supernatural plot.</p>
      <h3>9.3 Empire and writing back</h3><p><strong>Imperialism</strong> is the extension of a state’s power over other peoples and territories. Rudyard Kipling’s 1899 poem “<strong>The White Man’s Burden</strong>” frames colonial rule as a paternal duty; read it critically as an artifact of imperial ideology, not a neutral description. <strong>Writing back</strong> describes works that challenge colonial representations and reclaim voice or perspective. Chinua Achebe’s essays in <em>Hopes and Impediments</em> criticize how African characters and societies were represented by imperial literature.</p><div class="note"><strong>Exam distinction:</strong> Romantic nature can be a source of spiritual awe; literary Naturalism often presents people shaped by heredity, environment, and material forces. Neither idea is the same as “nature always rewards the strongest.”</div>

### Works and stories

- **Oliver Twist — Charles Dickens.** An orphan faces a cruel workhouse, escapes to London, and is pulled into Fagin’s gang of child thieves. Nancy helps expose the danger; Oliver’s origins are revealed, and he finds safety with people who care for him.
- **Hard Times — Charles Dickens.** In the industrial city of Coketown, schoolmaster Gradgrind demands “Facts” and suppresses imagination. His children Louisa and Tom show the emotional damage of treating people as machines; the novel criticizes utilitarian thinking and industrial inequality.
- **Dracula — Bram Stoker.** Through diaries and letters, Jonathan Harker, Mina, Van Helsing, and their allies discover that Count Dracula is a vampire moving from Transylvania to England. They track and destroy him before he can continue preying on Mina. The novel explores sexuality, invasion fears, science, and modernity.
- **Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde — Robert Louis Stevenson.** Lawyer Utterson investigates why respectable Dr Jekyll is connected to the violent Mr Hyde. He discovers that Jekyll’s experiment lets him separate and indulge hidden impulses, but Hyde grows stronger; Jekyll ultimately dies by suicide rather than face capture.
- **“The White Man’s Burden” — Rudyard Kipling.** A 1899 poem that frames imperial rule as a difficult paternal mission. Study it as an artifact of colonial ideology and ask how its language represents the colonized and legitimizes power.
- **Hopes and Impediments — Chinua Achebe.** An essay collection in which Achebe examines African literature, colonial representation, and the need to center African perspectives. It helps readers see how writing can answer inherited imperial narratives.
- **Wuthering Heights — Emily Brontë.** Visitor Lockwood hears housekeeper Nelly tell the story of Heathcliff and Catherine Earnshaw, whose attachment is shaped by class, pride, and cruelty. Catherine marries Edgar Linton; Heathcliff returns wealthy and pursues revenge across both families. Their children, young Cathy and Hareton, form a gentler bond that offers a way beyond the cycle, while Heathcliff dies consumed by his lost love. The novel is Gothic and psychologically complex, not a simple romance. <a href="https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/768" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Project Gutenberg full text ↗</a>

## Week 10 — Modernism, postmodernism, and the digital frontier

**Focus:** Fragmented form, inner life, and stories across media.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> When familiar beliefs and forms feel inadequate, writers reshape narrative to represent inner consciousness, social dislocation, and a media-saturated world.</p>
      <h3>10.1 Modernism and inner life</h3><p><strong>Modernism</strong> experiments with fragmented form and perception, especially amid rapid urban change and the shock of World War I. <strong>Stream of consciousness</strong> represents the flow of thought, memory, and sensation. <strong>Alienation</strong> is estrangement from other people, society, or oneself.</p><p>Virginia Woolf’s <strong>Mrs Dalloway</strong> follows Clarissa preparing for a London party over one day, while Septimus Warren Smith—traumatized by war—struggles with the world around him and dies by suicide. The parallel lives connect private memory, time, class, and war. James Joyce’s <strong>Ulysses</strong> follows Leopold Bloom and Stephen Dedalus through Dublin on June 16, 1904, with episodes loosely echoing Homer’s <em>Odyssey</em>; its interior monologues make ordinary thought part of epic form. T. S. Eliot’s <strong>The Waste Land</strong> is a fragmented poem of voices and allusions that evokes cultural dislocation after war.</p><p>D. H. Lawrence’s <strong>Sons and Lovers</strong> follows Paul Morel in a mining family divided by class and an intense mother-son bond; his efforts to love Miriam and Clara remain shaped by that attachment, and the novel ends with Paul moving toward an uncertain future after his mother’s death. In <strong>“Tickets, Please!”</strong>, wartime staffing leaves young women operating a Midlands tram line. Inspector John Joseph Raynor flirts with several conductresses; Annie becomes attached to him, then joins the women in confronting and beating him after he leaves her. When he is forced to choose, his selection of Annie does not repair the relationship: the ending feels bruised and unsatisfied. The story raises questions about gender, labor, desire, and revenge without giving a clean victory. <a href="https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/id/eprint/130206/1/WRAP_Theses_Concepcion_1993.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">University of Warwick research on the story’s revised ending ↗</a></p>
      <h3>10.2 Postmodern play with story</h3><p><strong>Postmodernism</strong> questions the idea that one stable story or style can explain everything. <strong>Metafiction</strong> draws attention to its own status as a made story. <strong>Pastiche</strong> combines or imitates styles and genres; it is not automatically mockery. <strong>Irony</strong> creates a gap between words, expectations, and meaning. Jorge Luis Borges’s <em>Labyrinths</em> gathers stories and essays that play with infinite libraries, mirrors, and invented worlds. Italo Calvino’s <strong>If on a winter’s night a traveler</strong> makes the reader a character, interrupting one novel with the beginnings of others. Jean-François Lyotard’s <em>The Postmodern Condition</em> is theory about knowledge and skepticism toward grand narratives, not a novel. <strong>Hyperreality</strong> is a theoretical idea about representations becoming entangled with what people take to be real.</p>
      <h3>10.3 Digital narratives</h3><p><strong>Ergodic literature</strong> requires meaningful effort or navigation from a reader, beyond simply moving through lines of text. <strong>Transmedia storytelling</strong> develops a story world across different platforms, with each medium adding material. <strong>Synthetic media</strong> is content generated or altered by computational systems. In <em>Black Mirror: Bandersnatch</em>, viewers make choices for programmer Stefan Butler as he adapts a fantasy novel into a game; branching paths make us question agency and control.</p><p><strong>“Berlin” note:</strong> The title is ambiguous without an author. A likely literary match is Vladimir Nabokov’s short story “<strong>A Guide to Berlin</strong>” (written in Russian in 1925). It has five brief sections—pipes, streetcar, work, zoo/Eden, and a pub—not one conventional plot. A narrator shows a bored companion ordinary city details, then imagines how future readers will value them as history. If your exam means a different work called “Berlin,” check the author or share the exact title. <a href="https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A5199c291-44e3-4cd7-a146-4226ec837e11/files/m99cd241f6ca3654aabc68f77dba22162" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Oxford research on Nabokov’s “A Guide to Berlin” ↗</a> · <a href="https://archive.org/details/englandmyengland00lawrrich" target="_blank" rel="noopener">D. H. Lawrence, <em>England, My England</em> collection containing “Tickets, Please!” ↗</a></p>

### Works and stories

- **Sons and Lovers — D. H. Lawrence.** Paul Morel grows up in a working-class mining family where his educated mother’s marriage to a miner has collapsed into conflict. Her emotional closeness shapes Paul’s relationships with Miriam and Clara; after his mother dies, he faces an uncertain choice to live independently.
- **“Tickets, Please!” — D. H. Lawrence.** During World War I, young women run a Midlands tram service while men are away. - **If on a winter’s night a traveler — Italo Calvino.** A reader begins a novel, but printing errors and interruptions lead to a sequence of different unfinished beginnings. The reader becomes a character, making the act of reading and the construction of fiction part of the story.

## Week 11 — The human condition: recurring conflicts

**Focus:** Mortality, society, nature, and the works that keep asking why.

<p><strong>Big idea:</strong> Comparing works helps us recognize recurring human questions while still attending to the different cultures and histories that shape each answer.</p>
      <h3>11.1 Universal struggles</h3><p><strong>Mortality</strong> is the fact of death; <strong>memento mori</strong> is a reminder to remember that fact. Gilgamesh’s grief for Enkidu turns his fear of death into a quest for immortality, while Hamlet’s soliloquy weighs endurance against the unknown after death. The <strong>human condition</strong> names shared dimensions of life—love, vulnerability, conflict, loss, hope, and the search for meaning—without claiming all people experience them identically.</p>
      <h3>11.2 Individual versus society</h3><p><strong>Individual vs. society</strong> (or “man vs. society”) is a conflict between a person’s beliefs or needs and a community’s laws, customs, or power. Sophocles’ <strong>Antigone</strong> buries her brother despite King Creon’s ban; she appeals to sacred obligation, while Creon defends state authority. Nathaniel Hawthorne’s <strong>The Scarlet Letter</strong> follows Hester Prynne, publicly punished for adultery, and the concealed guilt of minister Dimmesdale. George Orwell’s <strong>Nineteen Eighty-Four</strong> follows Winston Smith’s private rebellion against a surveillance state; he is arrested, tortured, and finally broken into loving Big Brother. In Suzanne Collins’s <strong>The Hunger Games</strong>, Katniss Everdeen volunteers for her sister and becomes a symbol of resistance to the Capitol. <strong>Conformity</strong> is adjusting to group expectations; characters may comply, resist, or do both.</p>
      <h3>11.3 Nature versus civilization</h3><p><strong>Nature vs. civilization</strong> explores conflict between human societies and the nonhuman world, or between instinct and social rules. Romantic writers often treat nature as sublime, restorative, or spiritually powerful. <strong>Naturalism</strong> is a literary movement that emphasizes how heredity, environment, and material conditions constrain people; it was influenced by scientific and Darwinian ideas. The Canvas self-check wording about nature as a “mechanistic force” and “only the strong survive” is not precise enough to identify one term with certainty. <strong>Naturalism is the likeliest literary-movement answer</strong>; “Social Darwinism” is a related ideology about applying competitive selection to society, not a synonym for literary Naturalism.</p><div class="note"><strong>Comparative answer:</strong> Name a shared theme, then identify one important difference between the works’ settings, values, or consequences. A universal theme is a starting point for comparison, not permission to flatten cultural differences.</div>

### Works and stories

- **The Scarlet Letter — Nathaniel Hawthorne.** Hester Prynne is publicly shamed for adultery and forced to wear a scarlet A, while Reverend Dimmesdale hides his role and Chillingworth seeks revenge. Pearl, Hester’s daughter, grows up under the community’s judgment; the novel tests the gap between private guilt and public morality.
- **Nineteen Eighty-Four — George Orwell.** Winston Smith secretly resists the Party’s surveillance and official rewriting of truth. He and Julia are arrested, tortured, and separated; after Room 101, Winston accepts the Party’s worldview and loves Big Brother.
- **The Hunger Games — Suzanne Collins.** Katniss volunteers for Prim and enters a televised fight to the death with Peeta. When the Capitol refuses to let both survive, they threaten to eat poisonous berries; the officials relent and name them co-winners. This defiance turns Katniss into a symbol of resistance, a useful example of individual versus society.

## Additional story-recall practice

The interactive quiz includes these supplementary questions about authors, plots, characters, and endings. They are not Canvas self-check items.

- **Week 1: A critic studies how the repeated image of a locked door changes across a poem. Which evidence-focused step should come first?** Answer: Describe the textual pattern, then interpret it. Start with observation and evidence before making a broader claim.
- **Week 2: A student claims that a character’s death criticizes wealth inequality. What should support this interpretation?** Answer: Specific scenes or language from the text. An interpretation becomes convincing when connected to textual evidence.
- **Week 3: What does Oedipus discover while investigating the murder that caused Thebes’s plague?** Answer: He himself killed his father and married his mother. The investigation reveals Oedipus’s identity and fulfills the prophecy he tried to escape.
- **Week 3: What does Aeneas leave behind when duty calls him toward Italy?** Answer: Dido and a private life in Carthage. Aeneas’s departure dramatizes the conflict between personal love and pietas.
- **Week 3: Which book by Marcus Aurelius is a collection of personal reflections rather than a novel?** Answer: Meditations. It records Stoic reminders about judgment, conduct, and mortality.
- **Week 4: What loss begins Gilgamesh’s quest for immortality?** Answer: The death of Enkidu. Grief makes Gilgamesh confront the fact that he, too, will die.
- **Week 4: Who tells Gilgamesh about surviving the great flood?** Answer: Utnapishtim. Utnapishtim recounts the flood and tests Gilgamesh’s ability to overcome human limits.
- **Week 5: Who leads Dante through Inferno?** Answer: Virgil. Virgil is Dante’s classical poetic guide; Beatrice guides him later in Paradise.
- **Week 5: What principle makes punishments in Inferno reflect the sins committed?** Answer: Contrapasso. The punishments symbolically correspond to or reverse the sinners’ choices.
- **Week 5: What are the three major fights in Beowulf?** Answer: Grendel, Grendel’s mother, and the dragon. Beowulf dies from wounds after killing the dragon with help from Wiglaf.
- **Week 7: What does Hamlet arrange to test whether Claudius murdered his father?** Answer: A play that reenacts the murder. Claudius’s reaction convinces Hamlet of his guilt.
- **Week 7: What question is Hamlet weighing in “To be, or not to be”?** Answer: Whether to endure life’s suffering or end life, and fear of what follows death. The soliloquy explores existence, suffering, and the unknown after death—not just hesitation about one task.
- **Week 7: Does Swift’s A Modest Proposal sincerely recommend eating children?** Answer: No; it is satirical irony criticizing exploitation. The calm economic voice makes the proposal outrageous to expose how the poor are dehumanized.
- **Week 7: What does Candide decide at the end of Voltaire’s satire?** Answer: To cultivate the garden. The ending favors practical work over Pangloss’s abstract optimism.
- **Week 8: What does Don Quixote mistake for giants?** Answer: Windmills. He interprets an ordinary landscape through the adventures in his chivalric books.
- **Week 8: Who travels as Don Quixote’s practical squire?** Answer: Sancho Panza. Sancho hopes for a reward and often grounds the pair in practical reality.
- **Week 8: Why does Jane Eyre leave Rochester after learning his secret?** Answer: He is already married, and she will not abandon her principles. Jane chooses self-respect and independence over a relationship that would compromise them.
- **Week 8: Who narrates The Great Gatsby, filtering the story of Jay Gatsby?** Answer: Nick Carraway. Nick is a participant and interpreter, so his perspective shapes what readers know.
- **Week 9: Who is the orphan at the center of Dickens’s Oliver Twist?** Answer: Oliver Twist. His workhouse childhood and exposure to Fagin’s gang frame the novel’s social critique.
- **Week 9: What Victorian Gothic story divides respectable Dr Jekyll from violent Mr Hyde?** Answer: Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde. Stevenson’s novella dramatizes secrecy and divided identity.
- **Week 9: How should readers approach “The White Man’s Burden” today?** Answer: As a historically situated poem that frames empire paternalistically. Recognize its imperial ideology rather than treating its colonial assumptions as neutral truth.
- **Week 9: What emotional response does Wordsworth’s sublime landscape often evoke?** Answer: Awe mixed with fear or humility. The sublime feels powerful and vast, beyond ordinary control.
- **Week 10: What do Clarissa Dalloway and Septimus Warren Smith’s stories share?** Answer: A single day in London shaped by memory, time, class, and war. Woolf’s novel juxtaposes a society hostess’s preparations with a traumatized veteran’s crisis.
- **Week 10: What event drives Gilgamesh’s quest and also shapes modernist uncertainty in the era after 1914?** Answer: Enkidu’s death; World War I. This item compares two different works and periods: Gilgamesh’s grief and the modern shock of war.
- **Week 10: In “Tickets, Please!”, what happens after the inspector abandons Annie?** Answer: Annie joins the conductresses in confronting him; his forced choice does not restore their relationship. The ending is uneasy and dissatisfied, making simple revenge or romance readings incomplete. Lawrence substantially revised it for the 1922 collection, so check your assigned edition.
- **Week 10: Who is the inspector pursued by Annie and the other conductresses in “Tickets, Please!”?** Answer: John Joseph Raynor. D. H. Lawrence’s story connects desire and gender power with women’s wartime tram labor.
- **Week 10: What city and date anchor Joyce’s Ulysses?** Answer: Dublin, June 16, 1904. The episodes follow Leopold Bloom and Stephen Dedalus over a single day.
- **Week 10: What five ordinary things structure Nabokov’s “A Guide to Berlin”?** Answer: Pipes, a streetcar, work, the zoo/Eden, and a pub. The short story turns everyday details into artifacts that future readers may value as history.
- **Week 10: What makes Calvino’s If on a winter’s night a traveler metafictional?** Answer: It makes the reader a character and interrupts one narrative with other beginnings. The novel draws attention to how stories and reading are constructed.
- **Week 10: What do viewers control in Black Mirror: Bandersnatch?** Answer: Choices that branch the story of programmer Stefan Butler. The interactive structure invites questions about agency and control.
- **Week 11: Why does Antigone disobey Creon’s order?** Answer: She believes sacred duty requires burying her brother. The tragedy puts religious/family obligation against state authority.
- **Week 11: What happens to Winston Smith by the end of Nineteen Eighty-Four?** Answer: The Party tortures and breaks him; he accepts and loves Big Brother. The bleak ending shows the state’s destruction of private rebellion.
- **Week 11: Who volunteers to take her sister’s place in the Hunger Games?** Answer: Katniss Everdeen. Her protective act becomes a public symbol that threatens the Capitol.
- **Week 11: What lesson does Gilgamesh carry home after his failed search for eternal life?** Answer: Mortality cannot be escaped; a meaningful legacy and community matter. The epic redirects him from individual immortality toward what humans can build and remember.
- **Week 11: Who is punished with a scarlet letter by her Puritan community?** Answer: Hester Prynne. Hawthorne’s novel examines public shame, hidden guilt, and individual life under social judgment.
- **Week 11: How is literary Naturalism different from Romanticism’s common view of nature?** Answer: Naturalism emphasizes material forces and constraints; Romanticism often emphasizes nature’s sublime or spiritual power. Both movements are diverse; this contrast is a useful starting point, not a complete definition.
- **Week 6: Who is the Roman poet who guides Dante through Hell?** Answer: Virgil. This review question revisits Week 5; Week 6 has no separate Canvas self-checks.
- **Week 6: Which epic follows a king’s grief-driven search for immortality?** Answer: The Epic of Gilgamesh. Gilgamesh seeks an escape from death after Enkidu dies.
- **Week 6: What does memento mori ask a person to remember?** Answer: That human life is mortal. The phrase means “remember that you must die.”
- **Week 6: What critical lens studies class, labor, and economic power?** Answer: Marxism. This review question revisits the opening weeks.
- **Week 12: Which World War is closely tied to the modernist rupture taught in Weeks 10–11?** Answer: World War I. This is a review question for the midterm checkpoint; Week 12 itself contains no Canvas self-checks.
- **Week 12: What is the difference between a dynamic character and an unreliable narrator?** Answer: A dynamic character changes; an unreliable narrator’s account is questionable. Character development and narrator trust are separate concepts.
- **Week 12: Which novel follows an orphan who leaves Rochester after learning his secret?** Answer: Jane Eyre. Jane chooses her independence and principles before returning later.
- **Week 12: What is the difference between metafiction and stream of consciousness?** Answer: Metafiction exposes its own construction; stream of consciousness represents thought flow. One draws attention to storytelling; the other represents inner thought.
