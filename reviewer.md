## Week 13 — Poetry: how words create an experience

**Big idea:** Imagery helps you sense a poem; rhythm helps you hear it; figurative language helps you interpret it.

### 13.1 Imagery and the five senses

Imagery is language that creates a sensory experience. It can describe unpleasant things as vividly as pleasant things.

| Type | Sense | Original example |
|---|---|---|
| Visual | Sight | A red umbrella floated down the flooded street. |
| Auditory | Hearing | The gate scraped and rattled. |
| Olfactory | Smell | Burnt garlic filled the kitchen. |
| Gustatory | Taste | The bitter coffee coated my tongue. |
| Tactile | Touch | The wet sleeve clung coldly to my arm. |

Additional terms you may encounter: **kinesthetic imagery** evokes movement; **organic imagery** evokes internal sensations such as hunger or exhaustion.

**Onomatopoeia** uses sound-imitating words: hiss, buzz, thud. It can contribute to auditory imagery, but auditory imagery does not require onomatopoeia.

**Hyperbole** is deliberate exaggeration: “I waited a thousand years.” **Simile** compares using “like” or “as”: “The room was like an oven.” **Metaphor** directly presents one thing as another: “The room was an oven.” A sentence can use more than one device.

### 13.2 Meter, rhythm, and scansion — the missing explanation

**Rhythm** is the movement of stressed and unstressed syllables as you actually speak a line. **Meter** is its underlying recurring pattern. A **foot** is one metrical unit. **Scansion** means marking and analyzing that pattern.

Say “a-BOUT”: the second syllable has more emphasis. Below, `u` means unstressed and `/` means stressed.

| Foot | Pattern | Sound | Simple example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iamb | u / | da-DUM | a-BOUT |
| Trochee | / u | DUM-da | GAR-den |
| Anapest | u u / | da-da-DUM | in the DARK |
| Dactyl | / u u | DUM-da-da | MER-ri-ly |
| Spondee | / / | DUM-DUM | STOP! WAIT! |
| Pyrrhic | u u | da-da | two weak syllables within a line |

Spondees and pyrrhics are usually discussed as local substitutions, rather than the pattern of an entire English poem. Stress depends on context and pronunciation; scan the whole line, not isolated words alone.

| Feet per line | Name |
|---:|---|
| 1 | Monometer |
| 2 | Dimeter |
| 3 | Trimeter |
| 4 | Tetrameter |
| 5 | Pentameter |
| 6 | Hexameter |
| 7 | Heptameter |
| 8 | Octameter |

**Meter name = foot type + number of feet.**

- Iambic pentameter = five iambs, usually ten syllables.
- Trochaic tetrameter = four trochees in the underlying pattern; a final unstressed syllable may be omitted.
- Anapestic trimeter = three anapests, normally nine syllables in a regular line.

An original regular iambic pentameter example:

`we WALK | beside | the STREAM | beneath | the STARS`

Read it with natural emphasis: `we WALK | beSIDE | the STREAM | beNEATH | the STARS`.

**How to scan:** read aloud naturally → mark stresses → look for repeating groups → count feet → name the meter. Do not force every syllable to fit perfectly: poets vary a pattern for emphasis.

**Blank verse** is unrhymed iambic pentameter. **Free verse** lacks a fixed recurring meter; it can still have strong rhythm. **Rhyme** is repeated sound, usually at word endings, and is separate from meter.

**Clarification:** The lesson's heartbeat comparison is a memory aid. Iambic pentameter is not literally a medical description of a resting heartbeat, and Shakespeare also wrote prose and other verse forms.

Supplementary sources: [Academy of American Poets: Meter](https://poets.org/glossary/meter), [Harvard: Guide to Prosody](https://poetry.harvard.edu/guide-prosody), and [University of Pennsylvania: Rhythm and Meter](https://writing.upenn.edu/~afilreis/88/meter.html).

### 13.3 Symbol, metaphor, and apostrophe

- **Symbol:** something concrete suggests a further meaning. A locked door might suggest exclusion, if the text supports that reading.
- **Metaphor:** one thing is understood through another: “Grief is a locked room.”
- **Apostrophe:** direct address to someone absent, something nonhuman, or an abstract idea: “O Death, leave us alone.” It is not the punctuation mark in this context.
- **Personification:** giving something nonhuman a human quality: “The wind complained.”

Interpret symbols from context. A rose does not automatically mean love in every poem.

## Week 14 — Drama: suffering, reality, and social change

### 14.1 Classical and modern tragedy

| Concept | Easy meaning | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| Hamartia | An error or misjudgment contributing to downfall | Often taught as “tragic flaw”; not necessarily an evil personality trait. |
| Hubris | Excessive pride or overconfidence | Can contribute to hamartia. |
| Catharsis | Emotional clarification, purification, or release associated with tragedy | Commonly linked with pity and fear. |
| Classical tragedy | Often portrays prominent figures confronting terrible conflicts | Oedipus is the lesson's ancient Greek example. |
| Modern tragedy | Can center ordinary people and social pressures | Willy Loman in Death of a Salesman. |

Do not reduce the difference to “ancient people have flaws; modern people only suffer from society.” Both personal choices and social forces can matter. Macbeth is a Renaissance tragedy, not an ancient Greek play, although this module groups it with high-status tragic heroes.

### 14.2 Realism and the absurd

**Realism:** believable dialogue, recognizable settings, psychological motives, social problems, and generally understandable cause and effect. Henrik Ibsen is a major pioneer.

**Fourth wall:** the imaginary barrier between performers and audience. Talking directly to the audience can break it.

**Theatre of the Absurd:** repetition, uncertain purpose, disrupted logic, and failed communication dramatize the difficulty of finding meaning. Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot is the key example. “Absurd” does not simply mean a funny or random play.

### 14.3 Drama as social criticism

**Bertolt Brecht's epic theatre** asks viewers to examine the social systems behind events. The **alienation effect**, also called the **distancing effect** or **Verfremdungseffekt**, interrupts automatic identification. Actors may address the audience, songs may interrupt action, or signs may reveal what will happen. The goal is critical awareness; it does not require eliminating all emotion.

**Lorraine Hansberry's A Raisin in the Sun** connects family aspirations with racism and housing discrimination. **Augusto Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed** explores ways spectators can actively investigate and challenge oppression.

## Week 15 — Global literature: duty, voice, and colonial power

### 15.1 Confucius and the Bhagavad Gita

**The Analects** is a collection of sayings and conversations associated with Confucius, compiled by followers. Its central question is how people cultivate ethical conduct and good relationships.

| Confucian term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ren | Humaneness / benevolence | Treating others with care. |
| Xiao | Filial piety | Caring for parents and honoring family responsibilities. |
| Li | Ritual propriety / appropriate conduct | Respectful conduct that sustains relationships. |
| Junzi | Exemplary, morally cultivated person | Leading through character rather than intimidation. |

Filial piety is richer than blind obedience; Confucian ethics also makes room for respectful correction. The module's broad East-versus-West comparison is a teaching shortcut: both traditions contain diverse views about individuals and community.

**The Bhagavad Gita** is a dialogue within the Mahabharata. On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, Arjuna hesitates to fight relatives and teachers. Krishna teaches him about duty, the self, disciplined action, knowledge, and devotion.

| Term | Easy meaning |
|---|---|
| Dharma | Duty, righteousness, and sustaining order; its meaning depends on context. |
| Karma | Action and its consequences. |
| Samsara | The cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. |
| Moksha | Liberation from that cycle. |
| Atman | The self; its relationship to ultimate reality is interpreted differently by Hindu traditions. |

**Acting without attachment** means doing your work responsibly without making personal reward the sole purpose. It does not mean careless work or indifference to harm.

### The yoga paths you asked about — added explanation

Yoga here means a spiritual discipline/path, not only physical exercise.

| Path | Main focus | Memory cue |
|---|---|---|
| **Karma Yoga** | Selfless action, without attachment to personal reward | Hands: serve. |
| **Bhakti Yoga** | Loving devotion to the divine | Heart: devote. |
| **Jnana Yoga** | Knowledge, discernment, inquiry into the self | Head: understand. |
| **Raja Yoga** | Meditation and disciplined mastery of the mind | Mind: meditate. |

This four-path framework is a common teaching arrangement. The Gita strongly develops action, knowledge, devotion, and meditation; do not assume every tradition uses an identical four-part classification. See [Hindu American Foundation](https://www.hinduamerican.org/hinduism-101/how/) and [Swami Sivananda: The Four Main Paths](https://www.sivanandaonline.org/?cmd=displaysection&section_id=425).

**If you actually meant types of karma:** a common Vedantic classification distinguishes **Sanchita** (accumulated karma), **Prarabdha** (karma already bearing fruit in the present life), and **Agami**, often discussed with **Kriyamana** (current actions generating future consequences). Terminology varies by tradition. These are religious teachings, not scientific claims about why an individual suffers. Source: [Sivananda: Three Kinds of Karma](https://www.sivanandaonline.org/?cmd=displaysection&format=html&section_id=1179).

### 15.2 Postcolonial literature

**Postcolonial criticism** studies how colonial power shapes literature, identities, language, and historical representation. It also studies resistance to those effects. It is not limited to books written after independence.

**Eurocentrism:** treating European experiences and standards as universally central or superior.

Achebe's **Things Fall Apart** portrays Igbo society as internally complex before and during colonial disruption. It shows rich institutions alongside injustices. Its protagonist Okonkwo is neither an uncomplicated hero nor a stand-in for every African person.

### 15.3 Rizal and Noli Me Tangere

The title means **“Touch me not”** and echoes John 20:17. Rizal uses disease as a metaphor for social problems under colonial rule. His novel attacks abuses by powerful institutions and also exposes hypocrisy and status-seeking among Filipinos.

| Character | Study connection |
|---|---|
| Crisostomo Ibarra | Educated reformer; initially hopes to improve society through schooling. |
| Elias | A persecuted man who understands oppression from below and presses for deeper change. |
| Padre Damaso | Abusive friar power; also Maria Clara's biological father. |
| Sisa | A mother devastated by injustice involving her sons Basilio and Crispin. |
| Dona Victorina | Colonial mentality, pretension, and rejection of one's own people. |
| Maria Clara | Love, constrained choices, and patriarchal/colonial power. |

Treat these as interpretive connections, rather than a claim that each character means only one thing. The novels contributed to nationalist consciousness; the historical causes of Rizal's execution and the revolution were more complex than a single novel directly causing both.

## Week 16 — Short stories, essays, and life writing

### 16.1 Chekhov and small emotional changes

A short story can imply a large life through a few carefully chosen details. **Epiphany** is a significant moment of realization. An **open ending** leaves important questions unresolved. **Anti-climax** replaces an expected dramatic high point with something quieter or less conclusive.

The module calls Chekhov's ending a **Chekhovian anti-climax**. Learn that wording for its self-check, while remembering that “open ending” describes its unresolved structure more directly. Chekhov was a major innovator; he did not single-handedly invent all modern short fiction.

### 16.2 The reflective essay

**Anecdote:** a brief story used to illuminate a larger point. **Creative nonfiction:** factual writing using literary techniques such as scenes, characterization, and imagery. Those techniques do not authorize inventing events and passing them off as true.

In a reflective essay, distinguish the **experiencing self** (the person acting then) from the **reflecting self** (the narrator interpreting it later). Ask whether the narrator admits self-deception and complicity.

In Shooting an Elephant, the narrator appears powerful because he holds the gun. Yet he acts against his judgment because he fears ridicule. The contradiction supports Orwell's criticism of imperial authority.

### 16.3 Memoir and truth

| Form | Usual focus | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Autobiography | Broad account of one's life | Also selective and subjective. |
| Memoir | A period, relationship, event, or recurring theme | Does not cover every year. |
| Biography | Someone else's life | Requires evidence and interpretation too. |

A **truth contract / nonfiction pact** is the reader's expectation that factual claims are made honestly. Memory can be incomplete; an honest writer should distinguish remembered experience, uncertainty, and verified evidence.

**Ethics of life writing:** whose privacy is exposed, whether portrayal is fair, what evidence supports accusations, and whether details are necessary. Emotional sincerity does not make a fabricated event factually true. Contrary to the lesson's contrast, autobiography is not automatically objective, and fiction can also cause real harm.

## Week 17 — Adaptation, AI, and trust

### 17.1 Print to screen

**Adaptation:** reworking a story into another form, context, or version. **Remediation:** representing or refashioning one medium through another. These overlap; remediation does not simply mean “an unfaithful adaptation.”

**Cinematic language / visual grammar:** camera framing, angles, movement, lighting, sound, performance, and editing create meaning. A novel can tell us a character's thoughts; a film may suggest them through a close-up, hesitation, shadow, or sound.

**Chiaroscuro:** strong light-dark contrast. **Recontextualization:** moving a work into a new setting or interpretive context. An adaptation can change events while preserving a central conflict, or preserve events while changing their meaning.

### 17.2 AI and literary authorship

**Generative AI** produces content such as text, images, and audio from learned patterns. **Large language models** are a text-focused class of models trained to model language sequences. Fluent output is not a guarantee of factual accuracy.

The lesson argues that lived experience distinguishes human writing from generated writing. That is a philosophical position to discuss, not a settled proof that a generated work could never have literary value. Separate questions about the text's quality, the reader's emotional experience, and the maker's experience.

**Simulacrum** (plural **simulacra**) is the expected Baudrillard term for a simulation/copy lacking a straightforward original referent. Its philosophical meaning is more nuanced than “any fake.”

Discuss training data through consent, attribution, compensation, and labor. The lesson's copyright question expresses a concern; whether a particular use is infringement depends on facts and applicable law, so do not treat every training use as automatically illegal.

### 17.3 Deepfakes and critical reading

**Deepfake:** synthetic or manipulated media using deep learning to convincingly depict or imitate people. **Hyperreality:** a theoretical condition in which representations and reality become difficult to distinguish. It does not mean all truth is literally impossible to verify.

**Digital/media and information literacy:** evaluating sources, evidence, context, and authenticity. **Misinformation** is false or misleading information regardless of intent; **disinformation** is deliberately deceptive.

A practical verification routine:

1. Pause before reacting or sharing.
2. Find the original source, full recording, and publication date.
3. Check independent, credible reporting and primary records.
4. Compare the clip with its full context; a genuine clip can be misleadingly edited.
5. Treat visual oddities and automated detection scores as clues, not proof.
6. If evidence remains uncertain, say so and avoid spreading the claim as fact.

Printed books can also contain falsehoods. Truth preservation is shared work by readers, journalists, archivists, educators, platforms, and institutions. Supplementary perspective: [UNESCO IBE on critical thinking and generative AI](https://www.ibe.unesco.org/en/articles/critical-thinking-and-generative-artificial-intelligence).

### Week 13 · Poetry

#### Stopping by Woods on a Snowy Evening — Robert Frost

**Scene:** A traveler pauses with a horse beside woods filling with snow. He knows the owner lives in the village and will not see him stop. The horse shakes its harness bells as if asking why they have halted at a dark, quiet place with no farmhouse nearby. The woods attract the traveler with their beauty and stillness.

**Ending and effect:** The traveler acknowledges the woods’ pull, then remembers promises and continues on. The repeated closing idea of miles still to travel lets the poem remain suspended between rest and responsibility. Its regular rhythm sounds almost like a lullaby, while the returning rhyme and the traveler’s obligations keep the quiet scene from becoming a simple escape. The poem has snow and a horse, not the apple imagery mentioned elsewhere in the lesson.

#### Wild Geese — Mary Oliver

**Movement of the poem:** Oliver’s speaker begins by addressing a listener who feels guilty, lonely, or unworthy of belonging. Instead of demanding perfect behavior, the poem points toward the body’s ordinary needs and the natural world: weather, landscape, and wild geese continue their lives beyond human self-judgment.

**Ending and meaning:** The geese’s call becomes an invitation to recognize that a person belongs to the larger living world even while feeling imperfect. The poem moves from inward shame to outward attention and connection. Nature is not a reward for being good; it is already part of the world a reader can hear and inhabit. Its direct, welcoming voice is a major part of its effect.

#### Ode to a Nightingale — John Keats

**Movement of the poem:** The speaker hears a nightingale singing and feels both pleasure and pain: the bird’s music is beautiful, but it makes human suffering and mortality more noticeable. He imagines escaping through wine, then through poetry, and mentally travels toward the bird’s song. The poem recalls figures such as Ruth and imagines the song reaching people across distant times.

**Ending and meaning:** The speaker briefly welcomes death as a way to stop suffering, then recognizes that the bird’s apparent immortality belongs to the song, not to a single living bird. The music fades as the bird flies away. He is left asking whether the experience was real or a dream. The poem’s movement from desire for escape back to uncertain human life is as important as its beautiful description of the nightingale.

#### Sonnet 18 — William Shakespeare

**What the speaker does:** The speaker begins by asking whether the beloved can be compared to a summer’s day, then lists reasons summer is unreliable: it can be too hot, too windy, or too brief. The beloved’s beauty is imagined as more constant than the season’s changing weather. The poem does not depend on a dramatic plot; its argument develops from comparison to a claim about time.

**Ending and form:** The final couplet says that as long as people read the poem, the beloved’s beauty will continue to live in its lines. The poem thus presents writing as a defense against time, while also making its promise depend on future readers. It is a Shakespearean sonnet of fourteen lines, usually in iambic pentameter, with the rhyme pattern ABAB CDCD EFEF GG.

#### The Raven — Edgar Allan Poe

**Story:** Late at night, a grieving speaker reads to distract himself from the death of Lenore. A tapping at the door leads to a raven entering his room and perching above the door. At first the speaker jokes about the bird and asks its name. The raven gives the same answer—“Nevermore”—to each question, but the speaker increasingly treats the repeated word as a judgment on his grief and future.

**Ending and effect:** The speaker asks whether he will ever reunite with Lenore and whether the bird will leave. The answer never changes, yet his questions become more desperate. The raven remains while the speaker imagines that his soul is trapped beneath its shadow. Poe’s regular beat, repeated sounds, and refrain make the speaker’s movement from curiosity to obsession audible.

#### Still I Rise — Maya Angelou

**Voice and movement:** Angelou creates a speaker who addresses forces that try to humiliate, silence, or distort her. The speaker imagines how an opponent might resent her confidence, then answers with pride, humor, and images of abundance. Refrains return like a response to repeated attacks rather than a single confrontation.

**Ending and meaning:** The speaker connects personal resilience with the history of ancestors who endured slavery and oppression. The rising image grows from an individual refusal to be diminished into a collective claim to survival and dignity. The poem does not deny violence; it refuses to let an oppressor define the speaker’s worth or future.

#### My Life had stood — a Loaded Gun — Emily Dickinson

**What happens:** Dickinson’s speaker imagines a loaded gun standing unused in a corner until an owner recognizes it, carries it away, and gives it a purpose. Together they hunt, watch over landscapes, and share an intense bond. The gun’s voice makes it seem powerful and active, but its activity depends on the person holding it.

**Ending and interpretation:** The final image imagines the owner dying while the gun continues to live, yet the gun cannot act without the owner. The poem can suggest agency, dependence, protection, violence, devotion, or a speaker’s relation to a powerful patron; no single reading settles it. The proper title is commonly identified by its opening words, “My Life had stood — a Loaded Gun,” and the author is Emily Dickinson.

#### Harlem — Langston Hughes

**Questions and images:** Hughes asks what happens to a dream that has been postponed. The poem does not answer immediately; it tests a series of physical images, asking whether the dream dries, festers, smells, or becomes heavy. Each image makes delay feel more bodily and difficult to ignore.

**Ending and meaning:** The final possibility is that the postponed dream may explode. That brief ending changes the poem’s quiet questions into a warning about pressure and consequences. Read “dream” in the context of blocked opportunity and racial injustice, not only as an individual wish. The poem offers no single explanation for what happens next; its force is in making the reader consider the cost of delay.

#### Twenty Love Poems and a Song of Despair — Pablo Neruda

**What the book contains:** This is a collection, not one long poem with a single plot. Across the love poems, a speaker describes desire, closeness, remembered intimacy, and the changing body through images of night, weather, landscape, and touch. Some poems celebrate an unnamed beloved; others already sense distance or separation.

**Ending and meaning:** The final “Song of Despair” turns toward absence and the pain of losing a relationship. The collection therefore moves through desire and memory rather than toward a conventional resolution. Its natural imagery is powerful but not merely decorative: the speaker uses sea, darkness, and seasons to make emotional states feel physical. The exact relationship between the poems and Neruda’s life should not be assumed from the lyric voice alone.

### Week 14 · Drama & performance

#### Oedipus Rex — Sophocles

**Story:** A plague devastates Thebes. King Oedipus promises to find who murdered the former king, Laius, because the city has been told that the killer must be found. The prophet Tiresias resists the investigation, then says Oedipus himself is responsible. Oedipus suspects a political plot. His wife Jocasta tries to calm him by describing the prophecy she and Laius once tried to defeat.

**Recognition and ending:** A messenger and an old shepherd reveal that Oedipus was taken from his birth parents as a baby. On the road years earlier, he unknowingly killed Laius, his father, and later married Jocasta, his mother. Jocasta kills herself; Oedipus blinds himself and asks to be exiled. An investigation meant to cleanse the city becomes a devastating discovery about identity, knowledge, and responsibility.

#### Macbeth — William Shakespeare

**Story:** Returning from battle, Macbeth and Banquo meet three witches who predict that Macbeth will become king. Lady Macbeth urges him to seize the crown, and he murders King Duncan while Duncan is a guest in his castle. Macbeth takes the throne but becomes increasingly afraid of losing it. He arranges Banquo’s murder, while Banquo’s son Fleance escapes. Macbeth orders the killing of Macduff’s family when he sees the nobleman as a threat.

**Ending:** Lady Macbeth is consumed by guilt and dies. Malcolm’s forces advance using branches cut from Birnam Wood, fulfilling another prophecy. Macbeth believes no one born of a woman can kill him, until Macduff reveals he was delivered by Caesarean birth. Macduff kills Macbeth, and Malcolm becomes king. The play shows ambition becoming tyranny through choices; prophecy shapes Macbeth’s imagination, but it does not make him innocent of his actions. [Royal Shakespeare Company synopsis ↗](https://www.rsc.org.uk/macbeth/)

#### Death of a Salesman — Arthur Miller

**Story:** Willy Loman returns home exhausted after a driving trip and can no longer sustain his career as a traveling salesman. He clings to the belief that being popular and well-liked guarantees success. His wife Linda tries to protect him, while sons Biff and Happy struggle with his expectations. Memories show that Biff once admired Willy, then discovered Willy’s affair in Boston and lost faith in him. Biff has since drifted through jobs and failed attempts to become successful.

**Ending:** Biff tells Willy the family must stop pretending, but Willy interprets the confrontation as proof that Biff loves him and might prosper from an insurance payment. He drives away and dies by suicide. At the funeral, Linda says the house is finally paid off, but no one comes to celebrate. Biff rejects his father’s dream of popularity; Happy vows to continue it. Miller makes an ordinary salesman’s suffering tragic while questioning the American dream Willy believes in.

#### A Doll's House — Henrik Ibsen

**Story:** Nora Helmer has secretly borrowed money and forged her father’s signature so she could pay for a trip that saved her husband Torvald’s health. She has hidden the debt and made small economies to repay it. When the bank employee Krogstad threatens to expose her, Nora expects Torvald to protect her. Instead, when he learns the truth, he panics about his reputation and tells her she is unfit to raise their children.

**Ending:** A letter removes the immediate threat, and Torvald wants to return to the marriage as if nothing happened. Nora realizes that he values his social standing over understanding her sacrifice, and that both her father and husband have treated her as a doll to please them. She leaves the house to learn how to live independently. Ibsen’s ending asks whether a marriage can be equal when one partner has never been allowed a full adult voice.

#### Waiting for Godot — Samuel Beckett

**Story:** Vladimir and Estragon wait beside a tree for a man named Godot, though they cannot agree exactly when he promised to come. They consider leaving, quarrel, reconcile, and pass time with jokes, memories, and small routines. Pozzo and his servant Lucky arrive; Pozzo gives commands while Lucky carries luggage and performs when ordered. A boy delivers the message that Godot will not come today but will come tomorrow.

**Ending:** The next day repeats the pattern, but Pozzo is now blind and Lucky cannot speak. The tree has a few leaves, while the men’s memories remain uncertain. The boy again says Godot will not come until tomorrow. Vladimir and Estragon say they will leave, but do not move. Godot never appears, and the play never identifies him conclusively. Repetition and unresolved waiting make the audience experience uncertainty rather than solve a conventional plot.

#### The Cherry Orchard — Anton Chekhov

**Story:** Lyubov Ranevskaya returns from Paris to her family estate, which is deeply in debt and may be sold. The merchant Lopakhin proposes dividing the land into plots for summer cottages, a practical plan that would preserve the family’s money but require cutting down the cherished cherry orchard. Ranevskaya and her brother Gaev cannot accept the proposal and keep hoping for another rescue. Their daughter Anya and adopted daughter Varya face different futures.

**Ending:** Lopakhin buys the estate at auction and announces that he will cut down the orchard. The family leaves, scattered to new arrangements and uncertain work. In the confusion, the elderly servant Firs is forgotten and locked in the empty house as the sound of axes begins. Chekhov combines comedy with loss: the family mourns a beloved place but has failed to act, while a new social class takes ownership.

#### Mother Courage and Her Children — Bertolt Brecht

**Story:** Anna Fierling, known as Mother Courage, pulls a supply wagon behind armies during the Thirty Years’ War and tries to support herself by selling goods to soldiers. She travels with her children Eilif, Swiss Cheese, and the mute Kattrin. War gives her customers and a livelihood, but each child is drawn into danger. Eilif is praised for a violent act during wartime, then later executed for a similar act when the rules change. Swiss Cheese is captured and killed after Courage delays too long bargaining for his release.

**Ending:** Kattrin hears that a town is about to be attacked and climbs onto a roof to beat a drum, warning its people; soldiers shoot her. Mother Courage buries her and then hitches herself to the wagon to follow the army again. She survives, but the wagon and the need to keep trading prevent her from escaping the system that has taken her children. Brecht asks how war turns survival and profit into part of destruction.

#### A Raisin in the Sun — Lorraine Hansberry

**Story:** The Younger family—Walter and Ruth, their son Travis, Walter’s sister Beneatha, and their mother Lena—wait for an insurance payment after the death of Lena’s husband. Each person imagines a different future for the money: Walter wants to invest in a liquor store, Beneatha hopes to pay for medical school, and Lena wants a home with room and sunlight. She puts a down payment on a house in the white neighborhood of Clybourne Park.

**Ending:** Walter gives money to his business partner Willy Harris, who disappears with it, including the portion intended for Beneatha’s education. A white representative, Mr Lindner, offers to buy the family out so they will not move into the neighborhood. Walter briefly considers accepting, then rejects the offer and chooses to move. The family leaves with the future uncertain. Hansberry ties dreams of home and self-respect to racial discrimination, housing segregation, and family conflict.

#### Theatre of the Oppressed — Augusto Boal

**What the book proposes:** Boal’s work describes methods for making theatre a place to analyze power, not only watch a finished story. In Forum Theatre, actors present a scene of oppression that ends badly. Audience members can stop the action, replace a character, and try a different choice. Boal calls these participants “spect-actors” because they shift between watching and acting.

**How to picture its purpose:** The rehearsal does not promise that a real social problem can be solved by one clever performance. It lets a group test strategies, notice obstacles, and discuss who has power before trying to act in life. The work is theatre theory and practice rather than one plot-based play. It turns the audience’s response into part of the form.

### Week 15 · Global great books

#### The Analects — Confucius and his followers

**What the book contains:** The Analects is a collection of sayings and conversations associated with Confucius and preserved by followers; it is not a continuous story. Confucius answers students’ questions about learning, government, ritual, family, and how to become a morally cultivated person. Some passages model a teacher correcting a student, while others show an ideal ruler leading by example instead of fear.

**Key ideas:** Ren means humaneness or care; li is ritual propriety and appropriate conduct; xiao is family responsibility or filial respect; junzi is an exemplary person formed through ethical practice. The passages do not reduce morality to blind obedience: respectful correction can be part of responsible relationships. Read the book as a set of linked ethical discussions, not as a single doctrine presented in one uninterrupted argument.

#### Bhagavad Gita — traditionally attributed to Vyasa within the Mahabharata

**Story:** On the battlefield of Kurukshetra, the warrior Arjuna asks his charioteer Krishna to place the chariot between the two armies. He sees relatives, teachers, and friends on both sides and loses the will to fight. Krishna answers his crisis through a long dialogue about the self, duty, action, knowledge, devotion, and the nature of reality. At one point Krishna reveals a cosmic form that overwhelms Arjuna.

**Ending and meaning:** Krishna teaches Arjuna to fulfill his dharma and act without attachment to personal reward, while recognizing the spiritual self beyond the changing body. The teaching does not make the battle emotionally simple; Arjuna still has to choose how to act amid painful obligations. By the end, he takes up his bow. The Gita is a philosophical and religious dialogue embedded in the Mahabharata, not a standalone war story.

#### Things Fall Apart — Chinua Achebe

**Story:** Okonkwo has earned status in Umuofia through wrestling, farming, and titles. He is driven by fear of resembling his gentle father Unoka, and that fear makes him harsh toward his family. The boy Ikemefuna lives with Okonkwo’s household and becomes close to Nwoye, Okonkwo’s son. When the Oracle orders Ikemefuna’s death, Okonkwo takes part despite being warned not to. Later, an accidental killing forces Okonkwo into seven years of exile in his mother’s village.

**Ending:** Missionaries and colonial officials arrive while Okonkwo is away. Nwoye joins the church, and new courts and institutions alter village life. On returning, Okonkwo wants to fight the colonizers, but his community is divided. He kills a colonial messenger, realizes the clan will not rise with him, and takes his own life. The District Commissioner reduces Okonkwo’s life to a possible paragraph in a colonial book. Achebe’s ending exposes how an outsider’s account can erase the complexity of Igbo society.

#### Heart of Darkness — Joseph Conrad

**Story:** In a frame narrative, sailor Marlow tells companions how he traveled upriver in the Congo to retrieve ivory trader Kurtz for a European company. Along the way, he sees exploitation, forced labor, and cruelty hidden beneath claims of “civilizing” work. Kurtz is famous as an eloquent agent, but Marlow finds him gravely ill and wielding near-total power over a local community. Kurtz’s report for a humanitarian society ends with a violent postscript.

**Ending and critical lens:** Kurtz dies after uttering “The horror!” Marlow returns to Europe and visits Kurtz’s Intended, who asks for his last words. Marlow tells her they were her name, withholding the truth. The story indicts imperial greed, but Achebe and other critics argue that Conrad also gives African characters too little voice and humanity. Reading both points together avoids treating criticism of empire as proof that every part of the novel escapes colonial assumptions.

#### Noli Me Tangere — Jose Rizal

**Story:** Crisostomo Ibarra returns to the Philippines after studying in Europe and hopes to marry Maria Clara and build a school. He is welcomed into a society shaped by colonial officials and friars, especially Padre Damaso, who humiliates him. Ibarra’s school project is obstructed, and Elias—a boatman who has experienced persecution—warns him that reform will not be easy. Maria Clara’s family history is tied to Damaso, though she and Ibarra do not know the full story at first.

**Ending:** Ibarra is falsely implicated in an uprising and pursued by authorities. Elias helps him escape and is mortally wounded; before dying, he asks the boy Basilio to build a fire. Basilio finds his mother Sisa, who has been driven mad by the loss of her sons, and she dies. Maria Clara enters a convent after being separated from Ibarra. Rizal uses private tragedies to show abuses in institutions and the suffering of ordinary Filipinos. [Project Gutenberg English text ↗](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/6737)

#### The Miseducation of the Filipino — Renato Constantino

**Argument:** Constantino argues that colonial education trained Filipinos to interpret their own society through American interests and assumptions. Schools could teach literacy and provide opportunity while also making colonial rule seem natural, elevating foreign culture, and detaching learning from local needs. Education, in this account, is never just a neutral transfer of facts; it shapes identity and political imagination.

**Conclusion and caution:** The essay calls for education that begins from Filipino history and serves Filipino communities, instead of treating the colonial model as universal. It does not mean that every school subject or foreign influence is inherently harmful. Ask who designed the curriculum, whose knowledge is treated as authoritative, and whether schooling prepares students to understand and improve their own society.

### Week 16 · Short stories & nonfiction

#### The Lady with the Dog — Anton Chekhov

**Story:** Dmitri Gurov, a Moscow banker who is dissatisfied with his marriage and treats women as brief distractions, meets Anna Sergeyevna while both are vacationing in Yalta. She is known as the lady with the little dog and is also unhappily married. They begin an affair, but Anna feels guilty and returns to her husband. Gurov expects to forget her; instead, he keeps remembering her and realizes that he has fallen deeply in love.

**Ending and meaning:** Gurov eventually finds Anna in her provincial town, and they begin meeting secretly in Moscow. Their public lives remain separate from the relationship that now feels most real to them. The story ends with the two trying to work out how they might be together, just as their difficulty becomes clear. Chekhov gives no neat escape or moral verdict: the lovers have changed, but the future remains unresolved. [Chekhov stories at Project Gutenberg ↗](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/13415)

#### Hills Like White Elephants — Ernest Hemingway

**Story:** At a small railway station in Spain, an American man and a young woman called Jig wait for a train. They order drinks and talk about the hills, the heat, and whether they should try a new drink. Gradually, their guarded conversation reveals that Jig is pregnant and the man wants her to have an operation, generally understood to be an abortion. He repeatedly calls it simple and insists that he wants only what she wants, while she asks whether their relationship can return to the way it was.

**Ending and meaning:** The couple never clearly announces a decision. The train arrives; the man carries the bags to the other platform, then returns. Jig tells him she feels fine, but the brief exchange does not settle what she will do or what will happen to them. Hemingway’s omissions make readers infer the conflict from repetition, pauses, translations, and the contrasting landscape. [University of Virginia digital text and edition notes ↗](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Hemingway/hemingway-hills?view=pageImages)

#### Shooting an Elephant — George Orwell

**What happens:** Orwell’s first-person narrator, a British police officer in colonial Burma, is called to deal with an elephant that has gone into a destructive state called must and has killed a local man. By the time he finds it, the elephant is quietly eating. The officer believes it is no longer a danger and does not want to kill a valuable working animal. A crowd has gathered behind him, however, and he feels they expect him to perform the role of a decisive colonial ruler.

**Ending and argument:** Afraid of looking weak before the crowd, he shoots. The elephant takes a long time to die, and the officer later admits that he acted partly to avoid seeming ridiculous. The essay turns one disturbing event into an argument about imperialism: the colonized are oppressed, but the colonizer is also trapped into acting a part to preserve authority. Read the human narrator critically; the essay does not give the Burmese crowd a full voice. [The Orwell Foundation: original essay ↗](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/shooting-an-elephant/)

#### Notes of a Native Son — James Baldwin

**The title essay:** Baldwin remembers the final months of his father’s life, their difficult relationship, and the older man’s bitterness shaped by racism. Baldwin’s father dies on the day Baldwin’s youngest sibling is born and as a Harlem race riot breaks out. At the funeral, memories of family conflict mingle with the larger violence of segregation. Later, while working in New Jersey, Baldwin is refused service in a restaurant because he is Black; in a flash of rage, he throws a glass and escapes before the consequences catch up with him.

**Ending and meaning:** Baldwin returns to his father’s life and sees how racial hatred can harden people and poison relationships. He refuses both to excuse his father’s cruelty and to treat hatred as a way forward. The book is a collection of essays, not a novel; the title piece combines memoir with an analysis of race, anger, history, and inheritance. [WorldCat record and publisher description ↗](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Notes-of-a-native-son/oclc/170299)

#### The Year of Magical Thinking — Joan Didion

**What happens:** Joan Didion’s memoir begins after her husband, writer John Gregory Dunne, suddenly collapses and dies at home. At the hospital, their adult daughter Quintana is already critically ill. Didion moves through the practical aftermath—medical decisions, funeral arrangements, clothes, documents—while her mind repeatedly returns to the moment of his death and to memories of their long life together. She describes the irrational hope that if she keeps his shoes, he may still need them and return.

**Ending and meaning:** The book moves backward and forward through memory rather than following a simple recovery story. Didion researches grief and illness while showing that understanding the facts does not make loss feel real all at once. “Magical thinking” names the bereaved mind’s attempts to bargain with reality. The memoir ends without presenting grief as solved: it records the gradual recognition that the life she shared with John has ended, even as memory continues. [Kirkus review and book overview ↗](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/joan-didion/the-year-of-magical-thinking/)

#### I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings — Maya Angelou

**Story:** Maya Angelou tells of Marguerite—called Maya—and her older brother Bailey, who are sent as small children to live with their paternal grandmother, Momma, in Stamps, Arkansas. The segregated town and racist humiliations shape their childhood. Maya is sexually abused by her mother’s boyfriend; after she tells the truth and he is killed, she becomes nearly silent for years. A kind teacher, Mrs. Flowers, helps her rediscover the pleasure and power of spoken and written language. As the memoir follows Maya between Arkansas, California, and back, it shows her trying to understand family, race, safety, and belonging.

**Ending and meaning:** In adolescence, Maya gradually becomes more confident, works as a streetcar conductor, and discovers her independence. At seventeen, she gives birth to a son and realizes she can love and care for him. The book closes on a beginning rather than a completed life: a young mother stepping into responsibility after a childhood marked by trauma. It is a memoir of racism and harm, but also of family, books, voice, and self-making. [Penguin Random House reading guide ↗](https://assets.penguinrandomhouse.com/book-resumes/AngelouMaya_IKnowWhyThe.pdf)

#### The Woman Warrior — Maxine Hong Kingston

**Shape of the book:** Maxine Hong Kingston’s memoir blends her memories of growing up Chinese American in California with family stories, Chinese legends, and imagined possibilities. In “No Name Woman,” her mother tells her of an aunt whose pregnancy outside marriage brought public punishment and erasure; Kingston imagines several lives the aunt might have had. “White Tigers” retells the legend of the woman warrior Fa Mu Lan and lets Kingston imagine a woman’s power. “Shaman” recounts her mother Brave Orchid’s life and work in China before immigration.

**Ending and meaning:** In “At the Western Palace,” Kingston’s aunt Moon Orchid arrives in America hoping to reunite with her husband, but he rejects her and she suffers a breakdown. The final chapter, “A Song for a Barbarian Reed Pipe,” returns to Kingston’s childhood struggle to speak and to her complicated relationship with her mother. By weaving legend, memory, and invention, the book shows how a daughter searches for a voice inside inherited stories—while leaving readers to notice that these stories are Kingston’s shaped recollections, not a single transparent record of Chinese culture. [Publisher record and contents ↗](https://books.google.com/books?id=9jNyfxW4a6QC)

### Week 17 · Literature in the digital age

#### Frankenstein — Mary Shelley

**Story:** Arctic explorer Robert Walton writes letters about his voyage and the exhausted traveler he rescues from the ice: Victor Frankenstein. Victor tells how his childhood curiosity became an obsession with creating life. After secretly animating a being assembled from human remains, Victor is horrified by its appearance and abandons it. The unnamed Creature learns language and human history by secretly observing the De Lacey family, but when he approaches them, they reject him. He asks Victor to make a female companion so he will not be alone.

**Turning point and ending:** Victor begins the second creation, then destroys it because he fears what the pair might do. The Creature retaliates by killing Victor’s friend Henry Clerval and, on Victor’s wedding night, his bride Elizabeth; Victor’s father dies soon after. Victor pursues the Creature across Europe and into the Arctic, where he dies aboard Walton’s ship. The Creature appears, grieves over Victor, and says he will end his own life before disappearing into the ice. Shelley’s frame narrative asks who bears responsibility when a creator refuses care, and how rejection can turn a longing for companionship into revenge. The Creature has no name; Frankenstein is the scientist. #### A Theory of Adaptation — Linda Hutcheon

**Main argument:** Linda Hutcheon studies how stories move from one medium or context to another. She treats adaptation as a recognizable reworking of an earlier work and as a creative process in its own right—not simply a test of whether a film is faithful to a book. An adapter selects, reshapes, and interprets; a viewer or reader may experience the adaptation with or without knowing its source.

**How to use the idea:** Hutcheon compares modes of engagement: telling a story through words, showing it through performance or images, and allowing audiences to interact with it. Each medium has different strengths and limits, so changes in point of view, pacing, scenes, or ending may be necessary or purposeful. When comparing a novel and film, ask what was transformed, what the new form can do, and how the adaptation’s historical audience and context affect its meaning. [Routledge edition overview ↗](https://www.routledge.com/A-Theory-of-Adaptation/Hutcheon/p/book/9780203095010)

#### Literature and Film — Edited by Robert Stam & Alessandra Raengo

**What the book is:** This is a scholarly collection edited by Robert Stam and Alessandra Raengo, not one continuous story. Its essays examine how literary works and films tell stories differently and how adaptations interpret their sources. The volume treats adaptation as a relationship among texts, media, viewers, and cultural settings, rather than a one-way transfer where the book is automatically superior.

**How to use it:** Film can communicate through framing, editing, sound, acting, and images; literature can use narration, interior thought, and verbal style. A useful comparison identifies the source and film’s choices, then asks what those choices do to character, point of view, genre, and cultural meaning. The collection offers different critical approaches, so do not treat one essay’s method as the only correct way to compare books and films. [Wiley: collection description and editors ↗](https://www.wiley.com/en-gb/literature-and-film-a-guide-to-the-theory-and-practice-of-film-adaptation-p-9780631230557)

#### The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction — Walter Benjamin

**Main argument:** Walter Benjamin asks what happens to art when photography, film, and other technologies can make many copies of something that once had a unique physical presence. He calls the felt uniqueness and historical presence of an original its *aura*. Reproduction weakens the artwork’s dependence on a single place, ritual, or owner, while making it easier for large audiences to encounter and circulate images.

**Why it matters:** Benjamin sees new possibilities and risks in mass media. A film can be edited, repeated, and shown to crowds, changing how people experience art and public life. Reproduction can broaden access, but it can also be used to shape politics. He contrasts fascism’s use of spectacle to aestheticize politics with the possibility of politicizing art. The essay is an argument about technology, culture, and power—not simply a claim that copies are inferior. [Modernist Commons: essay context and scholarship ↗](https://modernistcommons.ca/islandora/object/yale%3A665)

#### Simulacra and Simulation — Jean Baudrillard

**Main argument:** Jean Baudrillard examines a society saturated with signs, images, models, and media. A *simulacrum* is a representation that no longer functions as a straightforward copy of a stable original. When models and signs shape what people experience as real, Baudrillard calls the condition *hyperreality*. The book is a set of philosophical essays, not a conventional plot or a practical manual for identifying fake images.

**How to read it:** Baudrillard’s provocative examples ask whether representations merely describe reality or help organize it. Advertising, television, theme parks, and political spectacle can make the distinction between “the real” and its staged image difficult to maintain. His claims are deliberately sweeping and have been debated; use the key terms to analyze a specific example, and explain what the theory helps you see rather than repeating “everything is fake.” [University of Michigan Press overview ↗](https://press.umich.edu/Books/S/Simulacra-and-Simulation)

#### 21 Lessons for the 21st Century — Yuval Noah Harari

**What the book covers:** Yuval Noah Harari’s collection of 21 essays considers urgent political, technological, and personal questions. Its five broad sections address technological change; political challenges such as nationalism and community; hope and despair; truth and misinformation; and education, meaning, and resilience. Rather than telling one story, the chapters offer arguments and questions about a changing world.

**Central questions:** Harari asks how people can make sense of rapid change, big data, artificial intelligence, biotechnology, political division, and competing public stories. He argues that people need to distinguish reliable information from narratives that manipulate them and to keep learning as institutions change. Treat the book as a set of claims to evaluate: identify the chapter’s evidence and assumptions, then consider what other perspectives might challenge it. #### The Age of Surveillance Capitalism — Shoshana Zuboff

**Main argument:** Shoshana Zuboff describes a business model in which companies collect traces of ordinary life—searches, clicks, locations, and other behavior—and use data beyond what is needed to provide a service. She calls the extra material *behavioral surplus*. Firms analyze it to create predictions about what people may do; those predictions can be sold or used to influence behavior. Zuboff calls this economic system surveillance capitalism.

**Why it matters:** The book argues that the power to observe and predict people can become power to shape their choices, often without meaningful public understanding or control. Zuboff connects this asymmetry to privacy, autonomy, democracy, and the concentration of corporate power. Use the framework to ask what data a service collects, who benefits from it, what predictions are produced, and whether the design nudges behavior.
